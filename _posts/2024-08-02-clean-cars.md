*Written in August 2024.*

In a [recent article](https://kleinmanenergy.upenn.edu/news-insights/will-americas-clean-car-policies-persist/) for the Kleinman Center for Energy Policy, "Will America's Clean Car Policies Persist?", my co-author and I examined the United States' path towards zero-emission vehicles. We described how four cornerstone policies are accelerating the shift to electric vehicles (EVs): the EPA's emissions standards for passenger cars and light trucks, its standards for heavy-duty vehicles, California's Advanced Clean Cars II regulation, and federal EV tax credits under the Inflation Reduction Act. These policies also face legal, political and economic challenges.

While the article focuses on US policy under the Biden administration, the global context matters too. Many countries are actively pursuing clean-car policies. The European Union, for example, aims to cut CO2 emissions from new cars by 55% and from new vans by 50% by 2030, compared with 2021 levels. China, the world's largest car market, has invested heavily in EV technology and infrastructure: it had more than 13 million electric cars on the road in 2022, over half of the global total ([IEA](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2023/trends-in-electric-light-duty-vehicles)).

Comparing these efforts shows both common ground and differences. Most countries recognize the need to move to cleaner transport to mitigate climate change and improve air quality, but the pace and intensity of their policies vary widely, depending on economic conditions, energy resources and politics. In the US, the legal and political contest over clean-car policies continues, including court challenges to the EPA's standards. The outcome of these challenges and of the 2024 presidential election will shape the direction of US clean-car policy.

The Inflation Reduction Act's tax credits, while aimed at expanding domestic EV production, have also added complexity. The law's $369 billion for clean energy, including EV tax credits, has drawn both support and criticism, with concerns that its domestic-content requirements are protectionist and could distort global supply chains.

Even so, the shift towards electrification is well under way, with automakers investing heavily in EV development to meet growing demand. A successful transition requires coordination between governments, industry and consumers.

Several areas need attention to accelerate EV adoption:

- **Charging:** expanding the public charging network to ease range anxiety.
- **Cost:** lowering EV prices, which are often higher than those of comparable gasoline cars, through incentives and technological progress.
- **Batteries:** improving performance, cost and sustainability.
- **The grid:** investing in infrastructure and smart charging to meet higher electricity demand from EV charging.

The future of clean-car policy is uncertain, but addressing these challenges would help the United States and other countries move towards cleaner, more sustainable transport.
